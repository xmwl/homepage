Design lead · Jan to Apr 2026

Unified Navigation & Accessibility

Sprout's navigation had grown into two competing rails, it wasn't accessible, and our AI agent Trellis had no clear home. We had a hard compliance deadline and a big enterprise deal waiting on an updated VPAT.

I co-led the project with our engineering manager and PM, alongside a small crew of designers and engineers who all cared about getting this right. Together we built the rollout plan, lined up CX and Support, ran the exec conversations, and worked through the tradeoffs between design, engineering and accessibility.

Accessibility shaped the interaction model itself, not just the polish at the end. A few calls that defined it:

  • Hover as a shortcut, not a requirement. Mouse users can hover over an app icon to open its menu, but every menu still has a clickable button that works with a keyboard and screen reader, so no one depends on hover to get around.
  • Precise triggers. We tied hover to individual app icons instead of the whole rail and moved expand and collapse into the header, cutting accidental opens and clashes with tooltips.
  • One structure everywhere. Mobile and desktop share the same items in the same order, so nothing gets hidden or reshuffled for people using assistive tech on a smaller screen.
  • AI beside your work, not on top of it. Trellis moved from a drawer that covered about half the screen to a panel docked next to the page.
  • Snappier motion. A designer on the team benchmarked 16 products, averaging around 240ms and none over 350ms, against our 600ms default. I backed the case with session recordings and helped get faster timing tested.

Before committing, we tested the new direction against the old nav with users, and hover came out ahead for moving around within a section. Then we rolled out in stages: designers and our social team first, then R&D, then all of Sprout, then 10% of customers plus every new trial, and everyone within three weeks. A toggle back to the old nav let us measure real preference instead of guessing.

"We're approaching it not only to meet the current accessibility standards, but to set ourselves up so that we can bake accessibility into our design and development cycles."

Me, in Sprout's accessibility announcement

Results

  • 83% task success in research, above the 82% benchmark for the old nav
  • ~40% came back. People who switched back to the old nav chose the new one again
  • No engagement drop as core actions moved to their new home
  • WCAG 2.1 AA compliance, a public VPAT, and hundreds of accessibility fixes across the app (read the announcement)
  • Built to last: an accessibility health dashboard and automated a11y checks on every deploy

TakeawayBiggest lesson: accessibility decided early is cheaper and better than accessibility fixed late.

R&D-wide initiative lead · Aug 2026 to now

Burning Down Papercuts with an AI Coding Agent In progress

Small UX fixes always lose out to the roadmap. They pile up in the backlog and quietly wear down how the product feels. I wanted to see if an autonomous coding agent could clear them without taking engineering time away from bigger work.

I started by pushing the agent myself on frontend, backend and AI-product tickets. In one early test an engineer reviewed and merged the fix in about a minute, calling it something the team "would have probably never fixed." That was enough to make it a real program, and leadership named me the lead for an R&D-wide simplification push.

To make it repeatable I built a Claude skill that turns a vague problem into a ticket the agent can act on the first time. I wrote how-to guides for designers, got designers GitHub access, and set up a triage agent that checks incoming tickets and asks the reporter follow-up questions before handing off. Designers review on a test site and leave feedback right in GitHub. An engineer still approves every PR. The best part has been watching designers go from filing papercuts to fixing them.

Early results

  • 14 agent-built PRs across 3 repos in the first cycle
  • Designers shipping production fixes without waiting on engineering capacity
  • Spreading on its own. Other teams are building their own guides and skills on top of the process
  • Honest lessons fed back in, like the agent skipping our translation pipeline

What's next

We just wrapped the first cycle and are tightening the loop for the next one. Papercut tickets now get grouped and handed straight to the agent, and every PR ships with instructions for the reviewer. We're also fixing the gaps we found, like translations, and working papercut review into the engineering support rotation so it keeps going without someone having to push it along.

TakeawayThe agent was never the hardest part. The real work was writing tickets it could act on, bringing designers close enough to GitHub to review PRs directly, and giving engineers a process that makes those PRs quick to approve.

Co-lead, Product Design-wide · Jun 2026 to now

Web Design Playground

Figma prototypes don't behave like the real app, and the barrier for designers to build real UI was high. So we built a shared playground where designers and PMs use Claude Code to rebuild and prototype Sprout pages 1:1, using our real design system components. Just as important, it's meant to be shared. Everyone's prototypes live in one place where the team can browse, fork and riff on each other's work, instead of everyone prototyping alone in their own Claude or Claude Design session. The inspiration came from the work Brian Lovin has done on Notion's design playground, which he walks through in this podcast episode.

Why the playground first

We didn't start here. Earlier in the year we explored the Figma-side route to the same goal: giving AI tools the right context to design with our real system. I set up spikes to bring our component library into Figma Make through our design system's MCP, and Code Connect went through a pilot to map Figma components to code. Both kept running into the same wall. The problem wasn't the technology, it was our context. Code Connect mappings pointed at three different Figma libraries, and AI is only as good as the source it reads from.

The playground sidestepped that by putting designers straight into code with real components, so the team got value right away instead of waiting on a perfect pipeline. It was a deliberate tradeoff: impact for designers now over completeness later. In parallel, we're cleaning up the Figma side with our Design Systems team. We picked one source-of-truth library, we're making "migrated means code-connected" the rule, and we're putting governance in place so it doesn't fragment again. Next up is giving our AI coding agent direct access to Figma through MCP. The two efforts feed each other, since cleaner Figma context makes AI output better everywhere, including in the playground.

What I built

I co-led it with an engineering partner. I built the page templates and shipped features from user feedback, like a components browser, sorting and a production-accurate AI chat. I also built a "promote prototype" workflow that turns a playground prototype into a starting PR for the real app.

The tool was only half the job. Most designers had never touched GitHub, so I focused just as much on teaching the team. I ran hands-on jams, starting with setup and then a follow-up on branching and opening your first PR. I made a step-by-step FigJam guide and held regular office hours. I also set up processes so GitHub felt safe instead of scary. I got designers write access, and I pushed for prototype-only PRs to skip engineering review, which became a company-wide exception. Designers now share work through PRs and give feedback in GitHub comments, the same way engineers do.

Results

  • ~140 PRs to the playground in its first 3 months
  • A design team at home in GitHub. Designers who'd never opened a PR are now contributing and reviewing prototypes regularly
  • Hours, not days. An interactive query builder took about an hour, versus a full day as a static Figma component
  • Prototype to production. Playground work has become shipped design system components
  • Other product teams asking for their own version

TakeawayAim for "Sprouty enough," not pixel-perfect. When designers can get an idea 80% of the way there in real code, they stop explaining how something should work and just show it. They don't need to become engineers, they just need a safe place to build with the real thing.

Design director & vision owner · Dec 2025 to now

Building toward an AI-first Sprout

Sprout's product areas are siloed, and most pages are fixed and one-off. I've been leading the vision for a composable UI platform. It's a flexible foundation where both people and AI can assemble the right view for the moment.

I wrote the early platform strategy and tied a long-range vision to a concrete first slice. That slice is AI-generated insight stories that tell you what happened and what to do next, which can open into a workspace for taking action. I held the line on a key product principle: stories with a point of view, not another report builder you have to configure yourself.

A lot of the work is alignment. I demoed the foundation and flows to the exec steering committee, set up a cross-team channel to get several groups building on a shared visual standard, and when engineering worried the architecture was too complex, I reframed a simpler path as an experiment so we could deliver sooner. I also coached the lead designer on the team, and I prototype in code myself, building working flows in the playground so conversations with leadership start from something real instead of a static mock.

Where it's at

  • Platform foundation live in production
  • First AI-generated stories rendering in production internally, with customer interviews underway
  • One shared foundation. Other teams are building on it instead of shipping one-off versions

TakeawayThink big on purpose. A bold vision keeps everyone pointed in the same direction and gives the team something worth building toward. A small first slice people can see is what turns it into something real.
